# Copywriting

The method for static and text ads. Brand voice and no-gos still win where they are stricter (`../brand/guidelines/voice.md`, `../brand/no-gos/no-gos.md`). Video script structure is not covered here.

"The power of your copy is in direct proportion to the depth of your research." (Schwartz). Copy is how you communicate your understanding of the avatar back to them.

## The rules of copy

1. **Nobody cares about you, your brand, or your product.** People care about themselves. Frame everything through their problems, desires, and outcomes. Not "We've been making cleaning products for 20 years" but "For 20 years we've helped homes like yours clean without the chemical smell."
2. **Results are all that matter.** The market decides what is good. Never argue about copy; test it, and use the data to close feedback loops.
3. **Write how people talk.** Contractions, short sentences, sentences that start with "And" or "But". Read it aloud; if it sounds stiff, rewrite. Aim for a 6th grade reading level or below (check with a readability tool).
4. **Copy is never too long, only too boring.** Interested people read everything that stays engaging.
5. **Every sentence is a slippery slide.** End sentences with intrigue, open loops, curiosity gaps; each sentence leads to the next; no natural stopping place before the call to action.
6. **Research is 80% of the work.** About 2 to 3 hours of research per hour of writing. Mine the customer's exact language (reviews, forums, comments), competitors' claims (what has already been said), questions people ask (their questions are your objections), and the product's mechanism.

## What people value: the value equation

Value rises with the **dream outcome** and the **perceived likelihood** of getting it, and falls with the **time** it takes and the **effort and sacrifice** it requires. Copy should raise the first two and shrink the last two.

## Frames: New, Easy, Big, Safe

The four frames that create value in a line. Hit two or three strongly; forcing all four into one line reads like a checklist.

- **New / only:** discovery language, "first", contrarian, "just found"
- **Easy:** "without trying", "automatically", "just X", "easiest way"
- **Big / fast:** specific timelines and numbers
- **Safe:** social proof, "not your fault", credible validation

## Do not categorise yourself

The moment you compare yourself to an existing category you lose "new" and become an alternative. Do not say what you are "like". Do not borrow category language unless you are reframing it. Position as the first thing that does X, not another version of Y. Fernwood is not "an eco spray"; it is the pod that makes the bottle you already own clean like the one you gave up.

## Show, do not tell

Telling ("your kitchen will feel cleaner") triggers the logical, sceptical brain. Showing the change through a scene, an image, or a behaviour triggers belief. Show the moment life is different: what they stop doing, what they no longer notice (no window open after wiping the worktop, no second wipe on the glass).

## Body copy without fluff: Objection, Claim, Proof, Benefit

- **Objection:** what they are thinking or doubting, addressed head on (it can be implied)
- **Claim:** the counter statement, what is actually true
- **Proof:** evidence that validates the claim (data, testimonial, mechanism)
- **Benefit:** the emotional payoff, what life looks like after

Cycle it once per objection. Body copy can run several cycles. The angle file's Objections to Pre-empt and Proof Points sections feed this directly.

## Walk the awareness levels

Every ad enters at the reader's awareness level and carries them through each level after it. See `../strategy/awareness-levels.md`. If the sub-avatar is unaware, the hook must not name the problem.

## Hooks and headlines

The hook's job is to stop the scroll and create curiosity, not to explain. Save the explanation for the body.

**Shape.**
- Hooks: about 5 words (3 to 7), at most 2 lines on a phone, scannable in under a second; it should look like a headline, not a paragraph.
- Static image headlines: short, 6 to 8 words at most.
- Do not: write paragraph hooks, explain the product, list several benefits, go into educational mode, or give away the answer.
- Do: create a curiosity gap, lead with the mechanism or the contrarian angle, use discovery language.

**Two hook frameworks** (pick one per hook):
1. **Biggest thing happening now:** scale, urgency, magnitude ("hottest", "fastest growing", "biggest").
2. **Quirky:** a small, counter intuitive twist; it does not need to be world changing, just unexpected.

**The Four U's**, in priority order. Hit at least three strongly.
1. **Unique** (must be present): novel mechanism, contrarian angle, pattern break. Without it nothing else matters.
2. **Useful:** connects to a desire they actually have.
3. **Urgent:** happening now; time sensitivity.
4. **Ultra specific:** concrete numbers, names, places, timelines. Vague claims are ignored; specific claims are believed.

Different U combinations can produce several times the response, which is why each angle gets three distinct hooks.

**Three hook sources** per angle: one discovery led hook from the new mechanism; one "what they're not telling you" hook from new information; one that validates the pain in the customer's own words.

Fernwood examples for the angle "the refill pods clean as hard as the bottle you gave up":
- Mechanism: "One pod. Tap water. Streak-free glass."
- New information: "Most of your spray bottle is water."
- Customer's words: "Finally an eco spray that actually cleans."

## Proof: never fabricate

- Only use proof that is true for this brand, with its real source.
- Do not invent volumes, names, reviews, or stories; do not attribute a borrowed statistic to yourself.
- With little proof of your own, prefer credible third party data and mechanism proof over volume claims; percentages over thin counts.

## Static image ads

**Rules.**
1. Assume the reader does not know you and does not care about you.
2. The image must match and emphasise the headline (congruence).
3. Make them feel something; make them feel understood.
4. Be clear, concise, and simple.

**Process.**
1. Load the research, the target sub-avatar, and the product or offer file.
2. Write nine headlines. For each: name the proven structure it borrows from (below), say why it stops this avatar's scroll, and give two iterations of the same idea.
3. Pick the top three.
4. Describe each image (layout, subject, text call outs) so a designer or renderer can build it. Visual concept templates are in `prompts/concepts/visual/`.

**Proven structures** from winning image ads. Borrow the mechanic, never another brand's identity.

| Structure | Mechanic | Example shape |
|---|---|---|
| Absurd price comparison | Two things side by side with "vs", same price, surprising pairing | A takeaway pizza vs a short-haul flight, both the same price |
| Time comparison | Tie the time it takes to read the ad to a product fact | "It takes 3 seconds to read this ad. That's how long one pod takes to dissolve." |
| This is X, this is Y | Plain version beside the transformed version, two short labels | "This is tap water. This is your kitchen spray." |
| Double meaning | A familiar saying that lands two ways | A sports saying that also describes the product |
| Fake review from the enemy | A zero star review from the one who loses if you win | "Zero stars. Would not recommend. A single-use plastic bottle" |
| Cheeky taboo wordplay | A line that sounds illicit and resolves innocently | A line about something "too good to be legal" that resolves to the product |
| Idiom pun | A saying with one word swapped for the product | "Every cloud has a refill lining." |
| Exaggerated problem scene plus question | Someone doing something absurd to cope, then the question | Someone cleaning a window with a hairdryer and a sock, "Need a better way?" |
| Unexpected comparison with the premium option | The modest option beats the flashy one on a surprising measure | A plain pod that outlasts a designer bottle on refills |
| Asterisk twist | A bold claim with fine print that flips it | A celebrity-sounding endorsement whose fine print names an ordinary customer |
| Pun plus proof call outs | Idiom pun headline, product at centre, three or four benefit call outs around it | Pun headline over the starter kit, with "plant-based", "streak-free", "no harsh smell" call outs |
