# Belief Framework

Three elements determine three parts of every ad:

| Element | Determines | Question |
|---|---|---|
| **Desire** | Ad **content** | What does the customer want? |
| **Identity** | Ad **visuals** | Who does the customer want to be? |
| **Beliefs** | Ad **development** | What must the customer believe to buy? |

## Why Beliefs Matter

If your customer does not have BELIEF in your product, your mechanism, or your claims -- they will NOT buy. Desire gets attention. Identity creates connection. But belief closes the sale.

**Belief** is the customer's mental picture of the world they live in. Beliefs form a filter every ad must pass through or be rejected, so the ad must fit the customer's version of the facts. Use the customer's logic, not your own, to prove three things: the product works; people with their beliefs rely on it; nothing else meets their specific need as well.

## Belief Rules

- **Never violate an established belief.** Contradict the ideal customer's beliefs in the slightest and nothing you promise can save the ad. A Fernwood buyer who believes "eco products never clean as well" will not be won by "the greenest clean ever"; the ad has to start from that belief and prove the clean.
- **One justifiable promise beats a pile of promises.** Stacking promise on promise weakens the ad. Build believability behind one promise instead.
- **Channelled belief can beat desire.** If you can channel an existing belief onto the product, it can be more powerful than the strongest desire.

## Belief-Building Methods

Use these in sequence to build conviction:

### 1. Yes Train

Start with statements the prospect already agrees with. Each "yes" makes the next agreement easier. Build momentum before introducing your claim.

The questions let readers place themselves in the group you are talking to, not someone else. Once they are in the "yes" group, your recommendation carries weight.

### 2. Symptoms

Describe the prospect's situation so accurately they feel understood. When you name their symptoms precisely, they believe you understand the solution too.

### 3. Authority

Credentials, expert endorsements, certifications, years of experience. External proof that your claims are credible.

The shape: "I know you think this is true, but here is why it is false." Acknowledge the current belief bluntly, then show strong authority for the new one. You are not seeking agreement; you are loosening an old belief to make room for one that serves them better.

### 4. Logic

Step-by-step reasoning that makes the mechanism feel inevitable. "If A is true, and B is true, then C must follow."

## Belief Audit Questions

Use these when developing ads or landing pages:

- What are the current market beliefs about this problem?
- What does the prospect currently believe about products like ours?
- What are the most common objections?
- Which of our claims sound too good to be true?
- What would need to be true for a sceptic to buy?

## Identification Framework

Identity determines visuals. **Identification** is the customer's desire to act out certain roles in their life: how they define themselves to the world as a specific kind of person. It is a desire not for satisfaction but for recognition and expression. People do not just buy objects; they buy roles.

### How Identification Works

- Everyone has a self image. The ad paints the picture of who they want to become through **symbols of identity**, without stating it.
- The unspoken layer matters as much as the stated benefit: a refill pod bought to cut plastic also lets the buyer feel like the organised, thoughtful one in the house.
- The role must fit their current identity. A 1950s instant coffee launch sold as a time saver failed, because saving effort clashed with how homemakers of the time saw themselves; reframed around serving better coffee and having more time for the family, it took off.

Use these questions to get the visual world right:

- **Role:** What role does your product offer to your prospect? (e.g. "the prepared parent", "the sophisticated host")
- **Instrument:** How does the product become an instrument for that role?
- **Environment:** Where does my ideal customer want to be? (This determines setting/backdrop)
- **Appearance:** What does my ideal customer want to look like? (This determines the model/talent)
- **Feeling:** What does my ideal customer want to feel? (This determines the story arc)
- **Expression:** What self-image do my customers want to portray? (This determines tone and aesthetic)

### Identity and Sophistication

In a Stage 5 market, identification is the main response (see `marketing-frameworks.md`). New identity fails when it feels inauthentic, when the brand's behaviour contradicts the values it claims, or when it tries to appeal to everyone.

## Map of Consciousness Reference

Hawkins' Map of Consciousness provides a scale of emotional states from low energy (shame, guilt, fear) to high energy (courage, love, joy, peace).

In practice: ads often start at a lower emotional state (fear, frustration) to create recognition, then escalate to a higher state (courage, pride, joy) to motivate action. The landing page and ad must align on one desire, one avatar, one message -- and move the prospect up the emotional scale toward purchase.
